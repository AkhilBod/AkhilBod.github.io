Trying to never lose at rock, paper, scissors

Sep 2026 · Tell · webcam hand tracking

I was scrolling through reels when I came across a clip from The Mentalist where Patrick Jane, Simon Baker's character, plays rock, paper, scissors and simply doesn't lose. He reads the other person, knows what they're about to throw, and beats it every time.

It's a TV show, so of course he doesn't lose. But it made me wonder how close a computer could get. I had just used webcam hand tracking for a hackathon game, so I had the tools, and I had a goal: convince my roommates that I couldn't lose either.

The first version: reading habits

My first idea was the one Jane would use. People think they're random at rock, paper, scissors, but they aren't. Most have a favorite throw, many stick with a throw after it wins and switch after it loses, and plenty fall into patterns without noticing.

So the first version only looked at history. It tracked about three dozen of these tendencies at once, scored each one on how well it had predicted recent rounds, and played against whichever was working best.

Against bots that followed a fixed pattern, it almost never lost. Against me, it called my throw about 10 times in 16. That's well above chance, but it still got me wrong six times, which is nowhere near Patrick Jane.

A robot that doesn't guess

While looking for a better approach, I came across a Zack D. Films video about a robot built in Japan that always wins at rock, paper, scissors. It doesn't predict anything. It watches your hand with a high-speed camera, sees which shape your fingers are starting to form, and answers before you've finished.

That changed the question. Instead of guessing what someone would throw, I could watch them throw it.

Watching the hand

The second version used my laptop webcam and MediaPipe, which tracks 21 points on a hand in every frame. From those points it measures how far each finger is extended, which is enough to tell a fist from an open hand from two fingers out. More importantly, it catches the movement in between. If your fingers start opening on the way down, they're usually heading toward the shape you're about to throw.

The first attempt waited until it saw my fingers move and then picked its counter. It almost never lost, but only because its throw landed after mine, which is cheating. So I held it to the same rule as a person: it throws exactly on "Shoot," using only what it had seen before that moment.

Played fairly, it still won three of every four games that didn't end in a draw. Getting there took some tuning, which I did by recording every game and replaying old rounds through each change.

Turning it around

Beating me was a good demo, but the point was for me to win. So I turned it around: the laptop would watch my opponent's hand and quietly tell me what to throw, as a spoken letter in one earbud.

To practice without a second person, I recorded clips of myself playing as the opponent and had the app play them back in sync with the countdown, reading each one the same way it would read a live hand.

That's where the plan ran into me. It took me about 290 ms to react to a cue, and an opponent's hand gives very little away until right around Shoot. Paper, with four fingers opening at once, usually showed up around Shoot. Scissors and Rock didn't become clear until after it. Either way, by the time I heard the letter and moved, the round was over. When I looked back at my wins, more than half came from my resting fist happening to beat Scissors. The cues were barely helping.

What finally worked

The fix was to stop relying on fast reactions. If I hold a fist by default, I already beat Scissors and tie Rock. The only throw that can beat me is Paper, which is also the one the camera sees first. So during a round the coach only ever says one thing: "s," for Scissors, when it sees Paper coming.

I also brought the first version back, but moved it earlier. If an opponent has a favorite throw that's stronger than chance, the coach tells me the counter about a second before Shoot, while the countdown is still going and there's time to act on it.

Against my own recorded throws, which leaned heavily on Paper, the combined approach won 12 rounds, drew 2 and lost 5. That flatters it, since much of the gain came from exploiting my habits. So I also tested it against a generated opponent that picks every throw at random and performs it with one of my real recorded hands. Over 10,000 simulated rounds:

StrategyWinDrawLoss
Hold a fist, no help33.5%32.7%33.8%
Fist plus the "s" cue, at my reaction speed37.7%34.4%27.9%
Same, for someone who reacts in 200 ms42.8%35.9%21.3%

Against true randomness, nothing wins more than a third of the time, which is what the first row shows. The cue turns some of my losses into wins, and someone with faster reactions would get more. Real people are never truly random, so in a real game the habit model adds to that.

What's next

The real test is my roommates. The plan is to set the laptop on the table in a judge view that shows only the countdown and the score, with the cue in one earbud.

What surprised me most was where the limit ended up. The camera did its job, catching nearly every Paper with almost no false alarms. The limit was me. The robot in that video always wins because it reacts in milliseconds. I can't, so the version that works leans on people being predictable and only asks me to react when there's time to.

The code is on GitHub. It runs in Chrome with npm start.
