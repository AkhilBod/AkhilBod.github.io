Checking whether an LLM summary says what its sources say

Nov 2026 · Python, Qwen 2.5 7B, DeBERTa · github.com/AkhilBod/review-summarizer

Review summaries are everywhere now. You open a listing and there's a short blurb saying guests loved the location but mentioned some street noise. I wanted to know how often a blurb like that says something the reviews don't, so I built one and checked it.

Short version: the summaries were mostly true. The sources they pointed to often weren't. About half the time, a review the model cited as proof said nothing about the claim. And when I tried to improve the prompt, I accidentally taught the model to say half of Columbus was close to Ohio State.

How it works

I used the public Inside Airbnb data for Columbus, Ohio: 2,959 listings and 202,113 reviews. I picked 60 random listings with at least 30 reviews each and used the 30 newest reviews for each one. Names, emails, and phone numbers get removed before any model sees the text.

Everything runs on my laptop. A 7B model (Qwen 2.5, through Ollama) reads the reviews and writes up to 5 pros and 3 cons. For every pro or con, it has to list which reviews said it. That takes about 25 seconds per listing.

Then a second, smaller model checks the work. It's trained to answer one question: does text A imply text B? For each claim, I ask it whether each cited review implies the claim. If none of them do, the claim gets thrown out. This takes under 2 seconds per listing.

First run

The model wrote 347 claims across the 60 listings, and cited about 5 reviews for each one. Nine times it cited a review that didn't exist, like "review 34" when there were only 30.

The checker threw out 7.5% of claims. That sounded fine until I looked at the citations: only 45% of cited reviews actually said the thing. The typical claim was true, like "the host was responsive", but came with five reviews attached where two said it and three were just generally happy.

Before trusting any of this, I read 20 of the thrown-out claims next to their reviews.

  • About 5 were made up. "Comfortable beds" on a listing where nobody mentioned the beds.
  • About 5 were two things glued together where only one was true, like "spacious and well-decorated" when guests only said spacious.
  • About 10 were completely fine. The checker was wrong.

The worst one: the model wrote "Safe and friendly environment". The review it cited said exactly "Safe and friendly environment", then "Would recommend highly". The checker scored it 0.25 out of 1 and threw it out.

Fixing the checker

My first idea was that long reviews confused it, so I checked each sentence on its own. That made things worse: thrown-out claims went from 8% to 22%. A claim like "clean and close to downtown" usually gets each half from a different sentence, so no single sentence covers it.

The real issue was simpler. The checker was trained on full sentences, and the claims were fragments. "Safe and friendly environment" isn't really a sentence. So I started rewriting every claim as "The reviewer says: safe and friendly environment." before checking it.

That one change took the example from 0.25 to 0.98. "No air conditioning on hot days" went from 0.44 to 0.96.

CheckerClaims thrown outCitations that check out
Claim as written7.5%45%
"The reviewer says: ..."4.3%54%

For claims about just one thing, wrongly thrown-out ones dropped from 5.5% to 0.8%.

It's not free, though. I read 18 citations the new version accepted that the old one rejected. About 13 were right. The other 5 were too generous, like accepting "spacious and well-appointed" from a review that only talks about location and the mattress. So the fix swaps a lot of wrong rejections for a few wrong approvals.

Fixing the prompt, badly

63% of claims were two things joined with "and" or "or", and that's where most of the remaining problems were. So I added two rules to the prompt:

  • One fact per claim. Don't join things with "and".
  • Use the guests' own words. I gave an example: say "close to OSU campus", not "great location".

Glued-together claims went from 63% to 49%. Better, but not great for a rule I said pretty directly.

The example was the real problem. In the first run, 4 claims mentioned OSU. In the second run, 31 did. For 11 of them, not a single review of that listing mentions OSU or campus. The model wasn't using the guests' words. It was using mine.

The checker caught 8 of those 11 and missed 3. Leaving the OSU claims aside, the new prompt was slightly better: 58% of citations backed up their claim instead of 54%. On 60 listings, that's too small to call a real difference.

That's the main thing I took from this. I built the checker to catch the model making things up, and the first big batch it caught came from my own prompt.

Not redoing work

Each summary is saved with a fingerprint of the reviews it was built from, so a rerun skips listings that haven't changed. To see how much that saves, I compared the data from August 25 and September 25.

What I'd change

My hand checks were me reading about 40 examples. That was enough to find the fragment problem and the OSU leak, but not enough to trust any of these percentages closely. A real version needs a few hundred claims checked by a couple of people, and the cutoff score should come from that instead of a guess.

I'd also only show citations that pass the check, since the checker already knows which ones do. And I'd split glued-together claims in code before checking them, since asking nicely clearly didn't work.

Next I want to try a bigger model and see whether the citation problem goes away or just gets harder to catch.
