My app made you pay before you could practice

Oct 2026 · interviewsense.org

The first time you open Duolingo, it doesn't ask you to make an account. It asks what language you want, and then it hands you a lesson. You're translating "the apple" into Spanish before it even knows your name. By the time it asks you to sign up, you've already done the thing.

I didn't think much about that until I looked at my own app.

InterviewSense is a practice tool for technical interviews. You answer questions out loud, it grades you, and you get better. At least that's the pitch. When I actually dug into the numbers, more than half of the people who started a free trial never ran a single practice session. Most of the people who cancelled did it within an hour of signing up. They signed up, looked around, and left before trying the one thing the app is for.

The opposite of Duolingo

Here's what signing up looked like. You filled out a questionnaire about your target role, hit a paywall, started a trial, and then landed on a dashboard full of options. Behavioral, technical, system design, resume review. Pick one!

That's a lot of homework before you get anything back. Duolingo gives you the reward first and asks for things later. I was doing it backwards, and the dashboard was where people got stuck.

So I changed where new trials land. Instead of the dashboard, you go straight into a mock interview for the company you said you were targeting. No menu, no choosing. You're answering a question within a minute of starting your trial, which is about as close to "here's your first lesson" as I could get.

Making the first question count

Getting someone into a mock interview fast only works if the first question feels like it was made for them. Duolingo handles this by asking your level up front, so a beginner doesn't get thrown into the deep end and someone who already knows the basics doesn't get bored.

My questionnaire was supposed to do the same thing. You told it your target company and role, and it was supposed to pull real questions for that company. When I traced it, the bank of company questions was never actually getting filled. The code to build it existed, but nothing called it. So someone prepping for Google and someone prepping for Stripe were getting pretty much the same questions, right at the moment I most needed them to feel seen.

Now the bank gets built in the background the second you name your company, so it's ready by the time your first question loads.

Asking for less

The other thing Duolingo gets right is how little it asks before that first lesson. A handful of taps and you're in. My questionnaire was a lot longer than that, and every extra question is one more chance to close the tab.

So I'm testing a shorter version against the original. The goal is to find the point where cutting more starts to hurt, where the app knows just enough about you to make that first question land.

The number I watch now

I used to look at signups. Now the number I care about is how many new trials finish their first mock interview, because everything else follows from that. People who practice once come back. People who stare at a dashboard don't.

Duolingo figured out the first five minutes a long time ago. I'm building InterviewSense around them now.
